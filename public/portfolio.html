Seedr: a two-sided marketplace, from thesis to pivot to relaunch

Seedr is a discovery network where early-stage founders and investors find each other through verified proof of work, in place of pitch decks and warm introductions.

  1. Problem and thesis

    Who is underserved, and why

    Early-stage discovery runs on warm introductions. A founder with real work and no network stays invisible. An investor has no fast way to separate a builder from a good talker.

    Thesis: verified proof of work should be the trust primitive, ahead of the pitch deck and the referral.

    That thesis set the product scope. Every feature had to either help someone prove work or help the other side verify it. Anything else went to the backlog.

  2. Constraint and pivot

    Regulatory risk, checked before build

    The first model was equity crowdfunding. Before investing more build time, I stress-tested it against SEBI regulations and the IT Act. As designed, it had no legal path to launch.

    Trade-off I accepted: giving up transaction revenue in exchange for a product that could legally ship and start learning from users.

  3. MVP build

    Scoped tight, shipped solo

    I had no coding background and no technical co-founder. Waiting for one would have cost months, so I learned AI-native development with Claude Code and Cursor and shipped a working Android app in under a month.

    I owned the relational data model, authentication and API layer. Founders and investors get role-segmented onboarding, and row-level security in the database enforces what each side can see. Privacy was in the first schema, not retrofitted.

    For a stretch I also led a three-person team, with a tech lead and a go-to-market lead, on a daily sprint cadence against a single roadmap.

  4. Instrumentation

    Measure before you invite

    Before opening the beta I set up PostHog funnels, cohorts and session replays alongside Sentry error monitoring. The goal was to see activation and onboarding drop-off from the very first user, and to make every roadmap call traceable to behaviour.

  5. Retro: I built before I could distribute

    The call I got wrong

    I spent about seven months improving the app before solving how users would reach it. It went out as an unverified Android build, which put friction in front of every install. Installs stayed close to zero.

    The product was never the bottleneck. The path to the product was.

    Distribution was my riskiest assumption, and I tested it last. It should have been a week-one test with five real users, before anything was built on top of it.

  6. Iteration

    Cut time to value

    In November 2026 I rebuilt Seedr for the web. Trying it now takes one link. Go-to-market runs on zero paid spend through founder communities, including YC Co-Founder Matching and IIT networks.

  7. What I take forward

    Operating principles

    • Constraints firstCheck legal, platform and distribution limits before writing the first line of the spec.
    • Riskiest test firstFind the assumption that kills the product if it's wrong, and test that one before the comfortable ones.
    • Evidence over opinionInstrument from day one so prioritisation is a data conversation.

recruit.ai: a multi-agent screening engine in 14 days

Built in 2025 for the IIT Patna AI Product Management Challenge. Open the live demo

Problem

Startup founders screen applications by hand, slowly and inconsistently. Most applicants never hear back, which hurts the employer brand with exactly the people the company wants.

Solution

A webhook-driven workflow in n8n that routes each application through specialised LLM agents. Every agent returns JSON in an enforced schema, so downstream automation never has to guess.

Outcome

The system scored 100+ applications against a technical rubric. Founders get a ranked candidate matrix. Every applicant gets specific written feedback, accepted or not.

AI quality

I defined the evaluation rubric and structured outputs so model decisions stay consistent, explainable and auditable.

Hospital operations pilot

A platform for patient intake, care coordination and administrative handoffs, piloted in 2025.

What I did

Mapped clinical and administrative workflows to find the high-friction handoffs, then prioritised features by risk, frequency and operational impact. Designed for high-stakes use with role-based access, audit trails and clear error states.

What happened

I piloted it with a few nearby hospitals. Adoption stalled. Teams were anchored to legacy, template-driven processes that a small rollout could not displace.

What I learned

In institutions, change management and workflow fit decide adoption as much as the product does. Stakeholder buy-in belongs in the roadmap, next to the features.
